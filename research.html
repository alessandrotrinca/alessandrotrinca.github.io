Research Interests

I am a theorist tracing the origins of the first galaxies and supermassive black holes, from the earliest seeds to the cosmic giants we see today. I explore their birth, growth, and evolution from different perspectives, connecting theory with the new observational windows that facilities like the James Webb Space Telescope are opening onto the early Universe.

Seeding & quasar progenitors

Formation of the first black holes

Some of the brightest quasars we know were already shining when the Universe was less than a billion years old, powered by black holes a billion times the mass of the Sun. Reaching that size so quickly is difficult to explain, and the problem starts right at the beginning: we still do not know how the first black holes formed.

Several possibilities have been proposed, ranging from the remnants of the first generation of stars to black holes formed through stellar collisions in dense star clusters, or through the direct collapse of massive clouds of primordial gas. These different formation pathways produce black holes with very different initial masses and environments, and each comes with its own challenges.

I use numerical and semi-analytical models to investigate these different scenarios, following how the first black holes form and grow as the first galaxies emerge and evolve. I am particularly interested in understanding what distinguishes these pathways and in predicting observable signatures that could help us tell them apart. With new observations probing the earliest galaxies and quasars, we may soon be able to put these different ideas to the test.

Accretion history & the AGN population

How early black holes grow

A black hole cannot simply swallow gas as fast as it is supplied. The radiation released by material falling in pushes back on the gas still arriving, creating a natural limit to how quickly the black hole can grow. Start with a seed of a few hundred solar masses and let it grow at that limit, and it cannot reach a billion solar masses within the time available in the early Universe.

This makes the rapid growth of the first massive black holes a fundamental puzzle. I use numerical and semi-analytical models to investigate how black holes can grow beyond this standard limit, and to explore the conditions under which rapid, super-Eddington growth can occur. I am also interested in connecting these growth scenarios to the populations of active black holes we can observe with facilities such as JWST.

An important question is how we can tell different growth scenarios apart observationally. Rapid accretion can leave distinctive signatures in the emission from the gas around a black hole, affecting its luminosity, spectrum, and detectability across different wavelengths. By modelling these signatures, we can identify observables that reveal when black holes are growing at exceptionally high rates and use them to test how the first massive black holes reached such enormous masses so quickly.

First light

The rise of the first galaxies

When JWST looked further back than any telescope before it, it found more bright galaxies in the earliest epochs than models had predicted, with some already extremely luminous just a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. Something in our picture of how the first galaxies lit up is missing.

Several possibilities could explain this. Star formation may have been more efficient in the early Universe than it is in galaxies today. Some of the light may not come from stars at all, but from gas falling onto young black holes. The first stars may also have been systematically more massive than those forming today, since the low abundance of heavy elements in the early Universe changes how gas cools and fragments. These possibilities may all contribute, and understanding their relative importance is key to interpreting what JWST is seeing.

Semi-analytical models can help exploring how stellar populations and growing black holes shape the light from early galaxies, and to connect these predictions to observable properties such as luminosities and spectra. Current and future JWST observations, together with new data from future facilities such as the Extremely Large Telescope, will provide new ways to test these models and better understand the physical processes shaping the first galaxies.

LISA · Einstein Telescope · LGWA

Probing black holes with gravitational waves

Everything telescopes tell us about early black holes comes with a bias built in: we only see the ones that are actively feeding and bright enough to detect. Gravitational waves offer a different view, allowing us to detect an otherwise invisible population of massive black holes through their coalescences. When two black holes spiral together and merge, the signal depends on their masses and distance, not on whether they happen to be shining at the time.

The next generation of detectors, from the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna in space to the Einstein Telescope on the ground, will open a new window onto the black hole population across cosmic history, including the era when the first of them were forming. The number and properties of these coalescence events will depend on how black holes formed and evolved, providing an independent way to test our understanding of their origins.

Theoretical models will be crucial for making sense of the huge wealth of data coming in the next decade, enabling to connect individual detections to the broader picture of how black holes formed and grew across cosmic time.
